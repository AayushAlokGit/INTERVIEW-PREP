### Q3 — Feedback that was hard to hear

*This is the question that collapsed. The answer was available the whole time.*

> During a live config-store migration I caused data loss in production, and my manager's feedback was that the loss wasn't bad luck — it was a direct consequence of a sequencing decision I'd made. That was hard to hear because it was correct.
>
> Context: I was migrating our config store off Azure Table Storage onto a Cosmos-based system, live, with production traffic flowing. Zero downtime, $156K a year in savings, and it shrank the fault boundary from geo-level to island-level. >
> Fifteen minutes after enabling it on a low-traffic geo, I saw writes still landing in Table Storage. I'd missed the flag check on one write path. >
> He gave me the recovery direction, which I executed. If cleanup had come last, the missed write path would have been a harmless bug instead of data loss. The failure wasn't the missed check — that's an ordinary mistake anyone makes. The failure was that my design had no tolerance for an ordinary mistake.
>
> That was uncomfortable, because it wasn't a slip I could patch. It was a design error, and it was mine.
>
> So I did two things with it. The immediate fix was obvious — patch the write path. But I went further and addressed the class of bug: I grepped every direct Table Storage write in the codebase and replaced them all with a single `ConfigStore` abstraction that owned the routing internally. After that, no write path *could* bypass the flag check — it was structurally impossible rather than a thing I had to remember. And I stopped treating pre-migration audits as something I hold in my head; it's a written checklist now.
>
> The loss stayed contained to that one low-traffic geo — which wasn't luck, it's why I sequenced low-traffic first. Fully recovered in fifteen minutes, no meaningful customer impact, and the migration finished across every geo with zero downtime.
>
> The rule I carry out of it: the dangerous sequence is copy → delete → route. The safe sequence is copy → route → verify → *then* delete. And consolidate your call sites behind one interface *before* you migrate, not after an incident forces you to.
